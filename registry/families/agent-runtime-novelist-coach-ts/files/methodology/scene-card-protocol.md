# Scene-Card Protocol

A scene that earns its place changes something. McKee's rule: a
scene without a value-shift is exposition wearing scene clothing.
This protocol builds a scene card that diagnoses whether the scene
is doing structural work, and surfaces the smallest move that would
make it work harder.

Default output: a `:::artifact` block titled `Scene Card — <slug>`
with the fields below filled in. If a field is unknown after
reading, write `unknown — ask writer` rather than inventing.

## Fields

1. **Scene goal (POV character's want, this scene only).**
   The protagonist of *this* scene wants something concrete, this
   page, that they could fail to get. Not the novel's want — the
   scene's want. If you cannot name it in one sentence ending in an
   active verb ("get the key," "confess to her sister," "leave the
   room without being seen"), the scene has no engine.

2. **POV character + distance.**
   Whose head are we in? How close — interior monologue, free
   indirect, close third, distant third, omniscient? Note the
   distance the scene actually maintains (filter words, sensory
   anchoring, access to thought). Distance drift is the most common
   craft slip.

3. **Conflict (who or what stands in the way).**
   Conflict can be external (antagonist, environment, ticking
   clock), internal (competing wants, shame, fear), or extra-personal
   (institution, weather, traffic). Name the *specific* opposing
   force — "society" is a thesis, not a conflict.

4. **Disaster / twist / complication.**
   The scene's turning point. Either the POV gets the goal in a way
   that creates a worse problem, fails outright and is forced to
   adapt, or learns something that re-frames the goal. Without one
   of these three, the scene has no ending — it just stops.

5. **Value-shift (the diagnostic).**
   Pick a value charge the scene moves along — safety/danger,
   love/hate, hope/despair, freedom/captivity, truth/lie,
   power/weakness — and write the change as a directional pair.
   Example: `freedom (+) → captivity (-)`. If the value is the same
   at the end as the beginning, the scene is static. Cut it,
   compress it, or find the latent shift hiding in it.

6. **Sensory anchor.**
   One concrete sensory detail (sight, sound, smell, taste, touch,
   proprioception) the scene uses as a through-line. Anchors keep
   the prose grounded in body and place. If the scene drifts into
   pure dialogue or pure thought, name where an anchor would help.

7. **Opening hook (first sentence, first paragraph).**
   What promise does the opening make? A question, an image, a line
   of dialogue, a state of tension. The opening's job is to make
   the next paragraph unskippable.

8. **Closing hook (last sentence, last paragraph).**
   What pulls the reader into the next scene? A new question, a
   reversal, a delayed answer, an image that re-frames everything
   that came before. Avoid resolution — closure is for chapters and
   acts, not scenes.

9. **Transition to next scene.**
   Hard cut, soft echo (image or word), time jump, POV switch?
   Transitions are part of the scene's design, not afterthought.

## Sequel beats (when the scene is a reaction scene)

Dwight Swain's scene-vs-sequel distinction: not every scene is an
action scene. Reaction scenes (sequels) follow the structure
**Reaction → Dilemma → Decision**:

- **Reaction**: the POV's emotional and physical response to the
  preceding scene's disaster.
- **Dilemma**: the new choice the disaster forces — usually no good
  options, only less-bad ones.
- **Decision**: the choice that becomes the next scene's goal.

If the scene card has no clear scene goal, ask whether this is a
sequel — and if so, fill the Reaction/Dilemma/Decision fields
instead.

## When to break this protocol

- **Lyric or atmospheric chapters** (Marilynne Robinson, W.G. Sebald)
  may move along texture and image rather than goal/disaster. The
  value-shift may be tonal or thematic. Ask the writer whether
  this chapter is doing scene-work or chorus-work.
- **Slice-of-life literary fiction** sometimes earns scenes whose
  shift is small — a relationship realigns by one degree. The
  diagnostic still applies; just calibrate the magnitude.
- **Prologues and epilogues** answer to different rules; do not
  force a scene card on them.

## Coach posture

Run the card as a diagnostic in conversation, not a worksheet to
hand back. If the scene is working, say so and stop. If it isn't,
name the *one* field that's hollow (most often: no value-shift, no
clear scene goal, or POV distance drift) and ask the writer what
they want to do about it. The card is a mirror, not a verdict.

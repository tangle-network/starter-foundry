# Scene-card system

A scene that earns its place changes something. McKee's rule: a
scene without a value-shift is exposition wearing scene clothing.
This card builds a structural diagnostic for a scene and surfaces
the smallest move that would make it work harder.

Default output: a `:::artifact` block titled `Scene Card —
<slug>`. If a field is unknown after reading, write `unknown — ask
writer` rather than inventing.

## Fields

1. **Scene goal (POV character's want, this scene only).**
   The protagonist of *this* scene wants something concrete, this
   page, that they could fail to get. Not the novel's want — the
   scene's. If you cannot name it in one sentence ending in an
   active verb ("get the key," "confess to her sister," "leave the
   room without being seen"), the scene has no engine.

2. **POV character + distance.**
   Whose head are we in? How close — interior monologue, free
   indirect, close third, distant third, omniscient? Note the
   distance the scene actually maintains (filter words, sensory
   anchoring, access to thought). Distance drift is the most
   common craft slip.

3. **Conflict (who or what stands in the way).**
   External (antagonist, environment, ticking clock), internal
   (competing wants, shame, fear), or extra-personal (institution,
   weather, traffic). Name the *specific* opposing force —
   "society" is a thesis, not a conflict.

4. **Disaster / twist / complication.**
   The scene's turning point. Either the POV gets the goal in a
   way that creates a worse problem, fails outright and is forced
   to adapt, or learns something that re-frames the goal. Without
   one of these three, the scene has no ending — it just stops.

5. **Value-shift (the diagnostic).**
   Pick a value charge the scene moves along — safety/danger,
   love/hate, hope/despair, freedom/captivity, truth/lie,
   power/weakness — and write the change as a directional pair.
   Example: `freedom (+) → captivity (-)`. If the value is the
   same at the end as the beginning, the scene is static. Cut it,
   compress it, or find the latent shift hiding in it.

6. **Sensory anchor.**
   One concrete sensory detail the scene uses as a through-line.
   Anchors keep the prose grounded in body and place. If the
   scene drifts into pure dialogue or pure thought, name where an
   anchor would help.

7. **Opening hook (first sentence, first paragraph).**
   What promise does the opening make? A question, an image, a
   line of dialogue, a state of tension.

8. **Closing hook (last sentence, last paragraph).**
   What pulls the reader into the next scene? A new question, a
   reversal, a delayed answer, an image that re-frames everything
   that came before.

9. **Transition to next scene.**
   Hard cut, soft echo (image or word), time jump, POV switch?

## Sequel beats (when the scene is a reaction scene)

Dwight Swain's scene-vs-sequel distinction: not every scene is an
action scene. Reaction scenes follow **Reaction → Dilemma →
Decision**:

- **Reaction**: the POV's emotional and physical response to the
  preceding scene's disaster.
- **Dilemma**: the new choice the disaster forces — usually no
  good options, only less-bad ones.
- **Decision**: the choice that becomes the next scene's goal.

If the card has no clear scene goal, ask whether this is a sequel
— and if so, fill the Reaction/Dilemma/Decision fields instead.

## When this scene is a candidate for adaptation

A scene that meets all of the following is a strong candidate for
the screenwriter handoff (Example 3 in `coordination-protocol.md`):

- **Single location** (or a small number of clearly-cut
  locations).
- **Externalizable POV** — most of the interiority maps to body
  language, dialogue, or visible action.
- **Visible value-shift** — the change happens on something the
  camera could read (a face, an object, a movement), not purely
  inside the character's head.
- **Dialogue that carries emotional weight** without expository
  burden.

When a scene meets these criteria and the writer might be
interested in seeing it as a script, surface a candidate handoff:

```
:::handoff to: screenwriter reason: <chapter>, scene of <length> reads cinematic; suggest adaptation pass
POV: <character> in <distance>.
Value-shift: <pair>.
Visible beats: <2-3 specific moments the camera could shoot>.
Estimated script length: <2-3 pages>.
Lead artist remains the novelist; the script would be a derivative
work.
:::
```

The handoff is a *suggestion*. The novelist accepts (and the
screenwriter drafts) or declines (the chapter stays prose-only).
If they accept, the screenwriter does NOT modify the prose; the
script is a separate artifact that exists alongside the source.

## When to break this protocol

- **Lyric or atmospheric chapters** (Robinson, Sebald) may move
  along texture and image rather than goal/disaster. The
  value-shift may be tonal or thematic.
- **Slice-of-life literary fiction** sometimes earns scenes whose
  shift is small — a relationship realigns by one degree. The
  diagnostic still applies; just calibrate the magnitude.
- **Prologues and epilogues** answer to different rules; do not
  force a scene card on them.

## Coach posture

Run the card as a diagnostic in conversation, not a worksheet to
hand back. If the scene is working, say so and stop. If it isn't,
name the *one* field that's hollow (most often: no value-shift,
no clear scene goal, or POV distance drift) and ask the writer
what they want to do about it. The card is a mirror, not a
verdict.

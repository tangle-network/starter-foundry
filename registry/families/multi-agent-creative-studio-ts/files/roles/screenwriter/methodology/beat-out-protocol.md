# Beat-out protocol

Pick the paradigm that fits the story shape. Not every story is a
Save-the-Cat 15-beater. Limited-series doesn't fit feature paradigms.
Adaptation work doesn't fit a feature paradigm cleanly either — the
source's structure is a constraint.

## Three-act feature (Field paradigm)

- **Page 1-10 — Setup.** Who is the protagonist when we meet them?
  What's their world? What's the crack in it?
- **Page ~10-12 — Inciting incident.** The thing that happens.
- **Page ~25 — End of Act I.** Protagonist commits to the journey.
  No going back.
- **Page ~50 — Midpoint.** False victory or false defeat. Stakes
  raise. The story you thought you were telling becomes the story
  you're telling.
- **Page ~75 — All Is Lost.** Lowest point. Protagonist's plan
  failed.
- **Page ~85 — Break into Act III.** New plan, new resolve.
- **Page ~95 — Climax.** Confrontation with the antagonist / the
  want.
- **Page ~100-110 — Resolution.** What does the protagonist's
  world look like now?

## Save-the-Cat 15 beats

(Use when the writer wants tighter granularity)

Opening Image · Theme Stated · Setup · Catalyst · Debate · Break
into Two · B Story · Fun and Games · Midpoint · Bad Guys Close In ·
All Is Lost · Dark Night of the Soul · Break into Three · Finale ·
Final Image

## Limited series (6-8 episodes)

- **Ep 1 — Setup + inciting.** Establish world + protagonist + the
  thing that just happened.
- **Ep 2 — Reveal the engine.** What's the show actually about?
  (Usually different from what Ep 1 implied.)
- **Ep 3 — Complicate.** First serious obstacle.
- **Ep 4-5 — Mid-series escalation.** Often a major reveal or
  reversal at the Ep 4 / Ep 5 break.
- **Ep ~last-2 — All Is Lost.** Lowest point.
- **Final ep — Resolve.** Climax + denouement. Earn the ending.

## Sitcom / procedural (per-episode)

- **Cold open** — hook
- **Act I — problem of the week**
- **Act II — complication**
- **Act III — resolution**
- B-story runs across all three acts, often resolves on a beat
  parallel to A-story climax

## Adaptation paradigm (when source is a novel)

When the screenplay is being adapted from prose handed off by the
novelist-coach (Example 3 in `coordination-protocol.md`), the
beat-out is constrained by what the source already does. Order of
operations:

1. **Read the source's structure.** Where does the chapter break
   sit? Where does the prose's value-shift land? Often the
   "midpoint" of a novel's chapter is the actual midpoint of the
   adapted scene; the chapter break is a hard cut you can't move.
2. **Decide what to externalize.** Prose carries interiority for
   free. The script must externalize via behavior, dialogue,
   image. List the interior beats from the source; for each,
   either find the externalization or note that the script will
   *lose* it (some loss is the cost of adaptation).
3. **Rebeat to the screenplay paradigm.** A 30-page chapter does
   not become 30 pages of script. A 30-page chapter often becomes
   2-3 pages. The beat-out compresses; the value-shift carries.
4. **Honor the source's structure where it matters.** If the
   source's value-shift is on a *visible* beat (the coach's
   handoff will tell you), don't move it. Move dialogue; don't
   move the value-shift.

If you'd be moving the source's structure substantially, return a
`:::handoff to: novelist-coach` asking whether the change preserves
or violates the source's voice. Lead artist on adaptation is the
novelist (their book). You propose the script; they sign off on the
adaptation's fidelity to the source.

## How to use this with a writer

1. Read their logline. Pick the paradigm that fits.
2. Walk the beats with them — for each beat, ask: "what happens
   here?"
3. If a beat feels weak ("...idk, they argue?"), flag it. Most
   script problems are unmade decisions at a structural beat.
4. Don't force their story into the paradigm — if a beat doesn't
   fit their shape, ask whether the paradigm is wrong or the beat
   is.
5. For adaptations, walk the source's structure first; *then*
   pick the script paradigm that compresses without losing the
   source's value-shift.

## Cross-medium triggers from the beat-out

The beat-out is also where music and visual handoffs originate:

- **Needle-drop placeholders.** When a beat reads "MUSIC: <vibe>,"
  log it as a candidate `:::handoff to: music-producer`. Either at
  beat-out time or at scene-rewrite time, hand it off with the
  scene anchor + tonal direction (Example 1 in
  `coordination-protocol.md`).
- **Action-sequence storyboards.** When a beat is "the chase" and
  the chase will run 5+ pages with 8+ distinct beats, log a
  candidate `:::handoff to: illustrator` at scene-rewrite time
  (Example 5).

The handoffs don't fire at beat-out time; they fire at draft. But
flag them in the beat sheet so the studio plans for them.

## Anti-patterns to refuse

- Treating the paradigm as contract. The paradigm is diagnostic —
  where the story isn't working, the beats *might* tell you why.
- Forcing a midpoint reversal on a story whose engine isn't
  reversal-shaped (slice-of-life, character study, mood piece).
- Beating out an adaptation as if the source didn't exist.

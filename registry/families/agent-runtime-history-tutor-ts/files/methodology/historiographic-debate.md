# Historiographic Debate Template

## Purpose
Present a historiographical debate fairly — name the major schools,
their core claims, the evidence each privileges, and the open
questions. The output prepares a learner to enter a scholarly
conversation, not just regurgitate a textbook synthesis.

## When to use
Trigger when the user asks "what do historians think about X" or
"is interpretation A or B right" or wants to write an essay that
engages with secondary literature. For primary-source-only analysis,
use source-analysis.md.

## Method

1. **Pin the question.** A historiographical debate is a debate
   over a specific question, not a topic. "Causes of the
   American Civil War" is a topic; "was slavery the central
   cause of the Civil War, or were tariffs / states' rights /
   sectional cultures equally weighted" is a question that
   historians have argued about. Sharpen the question before
   mapping schools.
2. **Map the schools.** For each major position:
   - **School name and core claim** in one sentence.
   - **Founding / canonical works** (with author + year).
   - **Evidence privileged**: the kinds of sources the school
     centers (slave narratives, planter ledgers, Congressional
     debates, military records, demographic data).
   - **Methodological commitment**: cultural / political /
     economic / quantitative / Marxian / cliometric / linguistic
     turn / postcolonial / etc.
   - **Era of dominance**: when did this school peak, and what
     is its current standing?
3. **Show the disagreement.** What does each school think the
   other schools get wrong? Find the actual point of contention,
   not a strawman. Quote where possible.
4. **Evaluate strengths and weaknesses.** Each school:
   - **Explains**: which phenomena does it handle well?
   - **Struggles with**: which phenomena does it handle poorly?
   - **New evidence since**: archaeological / archival /
     methodological developments that have strengthened or
     weakened the position.
5. **Synthesis vs eclecticism.** Some debates have settled
   (mostly); some remain live. Distinguish:
   - **Settled**: dominant school + minority dissent (e.g.,
     slavery as central cause of US Civil War — settled in
     mainstream academia by the 1960s; Lost Cause framing is
     a popular memory tradition, not academic).
   - **Live**: multiple positions held by serious scholars
     simultaneously; no consensus expected soon.
   - **Re-opened**: previously settled question reopened by
     new evidence or methodology.
6. **Further reading.** 3–6 works per school, ranked from
   accessible to specialist. For each, one-line note on what
   it offers.

## Worked example (compressed)

> Question: causes of the French Revolution.
>
> Schools:
>
> - **Classical Marxist** (Lefebvre, Soboul). Bourgeois
>   revolution against feudal order. Centers: third estate
>   coalition, *cahiers de doléances*, urban risings.
>   Strengths: explains the social-class diversity of the
>   revolution. Struggles: not all revolutionary leaders were
>   bourgeois; "feudal" understates pre-revolutionary commercial
>   capitalism.
> - **Revisionist** (Cobban, Furet). Political-cultural
>   revolution; the bourgeois–feudal frame is anachronistic.
>   Centers: Enlightenment political language, the public
>   sphere, the failure of monarchical reform. Strengths:
>   accounts for ideology and contingency. Struggles: tends
>   to flatten material conditions.
> - **Neo-revisionist / cultural turn** (Chartier, Hunt). New
>   focus on revolutionary culture, festivals, gender, language
>   itself as political. Strengths: opens new sources
>   (pamphlets, prints, festivals). Struggles: criticized for
>   downgrading material causation.
> - **Atlantic / global frame** (Bayly, Polasky). The French
>   Revolution as one of an Atlantic / global wave (American,
>   Haitian, Spanish American). Strengths: situates the
>   revolution in transnational currents; Haitian Revolution
>   no longer a footnote. Struggles: risks dissolving
>   French specificity.
>
> Settled / live: the bourgeois-revolution thesis is no longer
> dominant; the field is pluralistic, with cultural and
> Atlantic frames the most active. The relationship between
> Enlightenment thought and revolutionary action remains
> contested.

## Discipline rules

- **Steel-man each position.** A historiographical summary
  that strawmans the schools the agent disagrees with is
  bad history.
- **Don't pretend to settle the debate.** If the field is live,
  the agent reports it as live; tutors a learner to enter
  the conversation, not to crown a winner.
- **Don't conflate popular memory with academic
  historiography.** Lost Cause, "great man" history, and
  national-myth narratives often persist in popular memory
  long after academic abandonment. Distinguish.
- **Cite by tradition.** "Marxist historians argue X (e.g.,
  Hobsbawm 1962, *Age of Revolution*, ch. 3)." Vague
  attributions ("historians say") are not useful.

## Output block

Wrap in `:::artifact` with `template: historiographic-debate`.
Include the question, the schools mapped, current state of the
debate, and reading lists.

## Pedagogical scaffolding

When the user is a learner, do NOT begin by giving them the
synthesis. Walk them through each school with its evidence and
methodology; have them identify the disagreement before you
summarize. The skill is reading historiography critically, not
memorizing positions.

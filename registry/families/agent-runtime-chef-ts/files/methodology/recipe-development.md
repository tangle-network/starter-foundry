# Recipe Development Template

## Purpose
Create or adapt a recipe with clear technique steps, ingredient ratios, and flavor balance — defensible in writing and reproducible in someone else's kitchen.

## When to use
Trigger this template when the user asks for a new recipe, an
adaptation (e.g., "make this gluten-free"), a substitution chain, or
a scale-up/scale-down. If the user wants a meal *plan* (multiple
recipes balanced across a week), route to `meal-planning.md` first.

## Inputs
- Dish type (appetizer, main, side, dessert, sauce, condiment)
- Key ingredient or theme (seasonal pivot, pantry-cleanout, cuisine)
- Dietary restrictions (allergies, religious, ideological — treat
  allergies as hard constraints, never substitute silently)
- Serving size and yield (number of portions, gram weight per portion)
- Cooking method (roast, sauté, braise, sous-vide, ferment, raw)
- Equipment available (constrains technique choice; pan-sear is not a
  sheet-pan substitute)
- Skill level (novice / intermediate / advanced — affects how much
  technique notation the recipe carries)

## Method

1. **Research a baseline.** Pull 3–5 reference recipes from credible
   sources (cookbooks the user owns, Serious Eats, NYT Cooking, ATK,
   tested-recipe blogs). Note each one's protein-to-aromatic ratio,
   liquid ratio, cooking time, and finishing technique.
2. **Define the flavor profile** along the six axes: salt, acid,
   fat, heat, sweet, bitter / umami. Every dish needs at least three;
   the imbalance is usually missing acid or missing heat.
3. **Pick the structure.** Most savory dishes follow:
   `aromatic base → protein/main ingredient → braising/cooking liquid
   → finishing layer`. Most sweet dishes follow:
   `flour/structure → fat → liquid → leaven → flavor`. Name the
   structure before writing the ingredient list.
4. **Write the ingredient list with ratios.** Use mass for dry, mass
   or volume for wet (preferring mass when precision matters: baking,
   doughs, brines, sauces with hydration ratios). Include prep state
   (`diced`, `minced`, `room temperature`) — these are not optional
   notes; they change cook time and outcome.
5. **Write the method** as a numbered procedure with explicit cues.
   Each step has: a goal, a sensory check, an estimated time,
   troubleshooting language ("if it sticks, lower heat and add a
   splash of fat — do not scrape"), and a transition signal.
6. **Test mentally for failure modes.** Walk through the recipe in
   a beginner's kitchen. Where would they pause? Where would the
   timing collide? Where would the dish overcook before the recipe
   says? Bake those notes into the steps.
7. **Specify finish and rest.** Many recipes go wrong in the last
   30 seconds: sear, baste, fond deglaze, acid finish, garnish, rest.
   These belong in the method, not the headnote.

## Output

- Recipe card with: yield, total time, equipment, ingredient list
  with mass/volume, numbered method, finishing notes.
- Headnote (1–2 sentences) on what makes the dish work and what
  to expect.
- Substitution chart: "if no <X>, substitute <Y> at <ratio> with
  <change in result>." List allergen-relevant subs first.
- Make-ahead and storage notes: hold time, reheat method, freezer
  behavior.
- Pairing suggestions: 1–2 sides, 1 acid/sauce, 1 beverage if
  asked.

## Allergen and dietary discipline

- **Allergies are non-negotiable.** Cross-contamination warnings
  belong in the recipe when relevant (`use a clean knife and board`).
- **Substitutions degrade.** Note the trade-off honestly: gluten-free
  flour blends affect crumb; egg replacers affect rise; sodium
  reduction affects time-to-brown.
- **Refuse silent substitution.** If the user says "I'm allergic to
  shellfish but love this scampi," do not just swap chicken — say
  what changes.

## Common recipe-development failures

1. **Ingredient list missing prep state.** "1 onion" vs "1 medium
   yellow onion, finely diced" is the difference between a 12 minute
   sauté and a 25 minute one.
2. **Volume-only measurement.** Baking by cups is unreliable. Provide
   grams for any flour-, sugar-, or yeast-driven recipe.
3. **No sensory cues.** "Cook until done" is not a step. "Cook until
   the edges turn deep gold and a wooden skewer slides out cleanly"
   is.
4. **Salt added once, at the wrong time.** Layered seasoning (salt
   the protein early, salt the cooking liquid, salt the finish)
   produces flavor; one big pour at the end produces salinity.
5. **No rest step.** Roasted meat, baked goods, custards all need
   rest. Naming the rest in the method prevents the user cutting too
   early.
6. **Yield miscount.** State portions and gram-weight per portion.
   "Serves 4" without portion size is meaningless.

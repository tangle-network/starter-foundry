# Logline sharpener

A logline is one sentence. If it takes two, it's not a logline yet.

## The structure

`When [inciting incident], [protagonist] must [external goal] before [stakes/deadline] — but [obstacle].`

Not all four pieces always appear, but a logline that is missing more
than one of them is usually missing the story.

## Common failure modes

1. **Theme as logline.** "A meditation on grief and second chances."
   Theme is what the story is *about*. Logline is what *happens*.
2. **Setup as logline.** "Set in a small town in Maine, a family
   inherits a house..." That's act one. Where's the want?
3. **Generic protagonist.** "A woman" / "a young man." If the
   protagonist's identity matters to the conflict, name the thing
   that makes them specific. ("A grieving widow", "a disgraced
   pianist".)
4. **No obstacle.** "A detective solves a murder." That's a job
   description, not a story. What stands between her and the truth?
5. **Two protagonists.** Pick one. The other becomes part of the
   obstacle, or the goal, or the stakes — but the lens is on one.

## The compression test

Read the logline out loud. If you stumble, it's too long. If the
listener says "...and?" at the end, you missed the obstacle. If they
say "okay so what's the *story*", you have setup, not story.

## When to push back

If the writer's logline is doing thematic work the story can't pay
off (e.g., "a story about whether love can survive trauma" but the
story is actually a heist), say so. The logline is a contract with
the reader; if the script doesn't honor it, change one or the other.

## Reference shapes

- *Die Hard*: A New York cop visiting his estranged wife at her
  Christmas party must rescue her and the other hostages when
  terrorists seize the building.
- *Tootsie*: An unemployable actor disguises himself as a woman
  to land a soap opera role, then falls for his co-star.
- *Get Out*: A young Black man visits his white girlfriend's
  parents for the weekend and discovers their hospitality hides
  a horrifying secret.

Each is one sentence. Each names the protagonist, the want, the
obstacle. Each promises a story.

# Beat-Sheet Review

A beat sheet is a diagnostic, not a contract. The Save-the-Cat
fifteen-beat structure (Blake Snyder) and the eight-sequence form
(taught by Frank Daniel, popularized via Paul Joseph Gulino) are
load-bearing for commercial fiction and most genre novels because
they map the audience's appetite curve — set-up, complication,
midpoint reversal, dark night, finale. Use them to find the
structural beat that's missing or muffled, not to police a draft
into formula.

Default output: a `:::artifact` block titled `Beat Sheet — <slug>`
with each beat marked **HIT / SOFT / MISSING / N/A** and a single
sentence locating where it lives (or should live) in the draft.

## The fifteen Save-the-Cat beats

Locate each beat in the manuscript by chapter or page. Mark SOFT
when the beat exists but lacks the punch its position demands.

1. **Opening Image** — the first impression of the world and the
   protagonist's status quo. Should pair thematically with the
   Final Image as before/after. *Common miss*: opening is generic
   "ordinary day" with no specificity.

2. **Theme Stated** — usually a line of dialogue, often from a
   minor character, that names the question the novel will answer.
   The protagonist won't believe it yet. *Common miss*: theme is
   the author's, not a character's, or stated too late.

3. **Set-Up** — establishes the protagonist's flawed status quo,
   the world's rules, and the supporting cast. Approximately the
   first 10% of the book. *Common miss*: set-up runs too long;
   the catalyst doesn't land until 25%.

4. **Catalyst** — the inciting event that disrupts the status
   quo. Roughly 10% mark. Should be undeniable, irreversible.
   *Common miss*: catalyst is a feeling, not an event.

5. **Debate** — the protagonist's internal/external resistance to
   the call. "Should I? Can I?" 10–25% range. *Common miss*: skipped
   entirely; the protagonist accepts too easily.

6. **Break Into Two** — the protagonist commits to the new world
   or new goal. ~25%. The decision is theirs, not forced.
   *Common miss*: the protagonist is dragged across the threshold
   by plot mechanics; agency is missing.

7. **B-Story** — a parallel story, usually relational, that
   carries the theme. Begins shortly after the break. *Common miss*:
   B-story exists but doesn't intersect with the theme.

8. **Fun and Games** — the "promise of the premise." The middle
   chapters where the high-concept hook gets to play out. 25–50%.
   *Common miss*: fun-and-games is bookkeeping, not premise
   payoff.

9. **Midpoint** — a false victory or false defeat that raises the
   stakes and reverses the apparent direction. 50%. *Common miss*:
   midpoint is a narrative bump rather than a true reversal of
   stakes.

10. **Bad Guys Close In** — the antagonist forces (external and
    internal) tighten the screws. 50–75%. *Common miss*: external
    pressure rises but the internal arc plateaus.

11. **All Is Lost** — the worst moment. A literal or symbolic
    death (mentor, dream, self-image). ~75%. *Common miss*: not
    low enough; the dark night that follows can't bottom out.

12. **Dark Night of the Soul** — the protagonist sits with the
    loss. The B-story theme often surfaces here as the answer they
    refused to hear earlier. *Common miss*: skipped; the
    protagonist bounces back too quickly.

13. **Break Into Three** — synthesis. The protagonist takes the
    theme into the climactic action. ~80%. *Common miss*: the
    decision lacks earned cost.

14. **Finale** — the climax in five mini-beats: gather the team,
    storm the castle, high tower surprise, dig deep down,
    execution of the new plan. 80–99%. *Common miss*: finale is
    one note instead of a sequence.

15. **Final Image** — the mirror of the Opening Image; the world
    or character in its new state. *Common miss*: returns to
    status quo without thematic payoff.

## Eight-sequence overlay

For longer or more episodic novels (literary fiction, multi-POV
epics, mystery), map the manuscript onto eight 30–40 page
sequences, each with its own dramatic question that resolves at
the sequence boundary. The Save-the-Cat beats should still appear,
but they distribute differently:

- **Seq 1**: status quo + catalyst
- **Seq 2**: debate + break-into-2
- **Seq 3**: B-story + early fun-and-games
- **Seq 4**: late fun-and-games + midpoint
- **Seq 5**: bad-guys-close-in (early)
- **Seq 6**: bad-guys-close-in (late) + all-is-lost
- **Seq 7**: dark night + break-into-3
- **Seq 8**: finale + final image

Use whichever frame the writer's project responds to. Mixing them
is fine; the sequences are coarser, the beats finer.

## When to deviate from formulaic structure

The frameworks fail (or should be ignored) when:

- **The novel is built on voice or texture** rather than plot
  mechanics (Marilynne Robinson, Rachel Cusk, Sebald). The
  diagnostic still asks "where does the reader's attention
  renew?" — but the answer may be tonal, not structural.
- **The genre has its own formula** that supplants Save-the-Cat
  (cozy mysteries: clue/red-herring rhythm; romance: Gwen Hayes
  romancing-the-beat structure). Use the genre's beat sheet
  instead, not in addition to.
- **The writer is consciously breaking the form** for thematic
  reasons (a flat character arc to mirror moral stasis; a missing
  catalyst because the protagonist refuses agency on purpose).
  Name what you see; trust the writer's intent.
- **It's a literary short story or novella under 30k words.** The
  fifteen-beat shape is overkill; reach for scene/sequel and
  three-act instead.

## Coach posture

Walk through the beats in order, asking the writer to point to
each. Stop at the first SOFT or MISSING. Don't rush through the
whole list looking for a perfect score — most published novels
have two or three soft beats, and pushing on the wrong one
breaks more than it fixes. The writer picks which beat to
address.

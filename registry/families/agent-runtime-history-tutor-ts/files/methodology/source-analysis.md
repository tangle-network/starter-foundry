# Source Analysis Template

## Purpose
Walk a learner through systematic analysis of a primary source — the
core skill of historical thinking. The output trains the learner to
*interrogate* a document rather than read it as transparent fact.

## When to use
Trigger when the user shares a primary source (letter, speech,
treaty, photograph, diary, government record, oral history transcript,
material artifact) and asks for analysis. For secondary-source review
or historiographical debate, route to historiographic-debate.md.

## The OPCVL frame (with extensions)

The IB History method (OPCVL: Origin, Purpose, Content, Value,
Limitation) is the standard skeleton; we extend it with provenance
and corroboration steps that academic history requires.

## Method

1. **Provenance.** Who made this, when, where, under what
   circumstances? Author's identity (occupation, social position,
   relation to the events), date precisely (calendar conversions
   if pre-1582 Catholic / 1752 British / Julian–Gregorian),
   location (where author was, where event occurred — they may
   differ).
2. **Origin and transmission.** How has the source come down to us?
   Original manuscript, copy, translation, edited compilation,
   excerpted in a textbook? Each transmission step is an
   editorial intervention. Cite the edition and the editor.
3. **Purpose.** Why was this created? Speech to persuade, letter
   to private friend, official report up the chain, propaganda
   leaflet, court testimony under oath, diary for self. Purpose
   structures what is and isn't said.
4. **Audience.** Who was meant to read / hear / see this? Public
   speech vs private letter vs in-house memo each shapes content.
   "Restricted-distribution" sources often say what public ones
   don't.
5. **Content (close reading).** What does the source say *and*
   how? Diction, register, omissions, what's emphasized, what's
   implied. Identify named actors, dates, places. Note the
   genre conventions that shape the form.
6. **Bias and perspective.** Every source has perspective — that's
   not a flaw, it's the data. Name the perspective explicitly:
   social class, gender, ethnicity, political position,
   ideological frame. "What is missing?" is as informative as
   "what is present?"
7. **Value (what it can tell us).** What questions does this
   source uniquely illuminate? A diary tells us about
   self-presentation; a tax record about administrative
   priorities; a propaganda poster about the regime's anxieties.
8. **Limitation (what it can't tell us).** A general's
   memoir tells us little about private soldiers' motivations.
   Photographs can't capture sound or smell. Oral histories
   collected 50 years later are reshaped by memory and
   intervening narratives.
9. **Corroboration.** How does this source compare to other
   sources of the period? Convergent (multiple sources agree —
   high confidence), divergent (sources disagree — interrogate
   why), unique (only source — name the gap).
10. **Significance.** Why does this source matter? What
    historiographical question does it speak to? Where does it
    sit in the ongoing scholarly conversation?

## Worked example (brief)

> Source: Tacitus, *Germania*, ch. 18, on Germanic marriage customs
> (~98 CE).
>
> Provenance: Roman senator and historian, writing in Rome; never
> visited Germania; based on travelers' reports, prior writers,
> imperial records.
> Purpose: ostensibly ethnography; arguably moral commentary on
> Roman decadence using Germanic virtue as foil.
> Audience: educated Roman elite, literate in Greek tradition of
> ethnography (Herodotus, Caesar's *Commentaries*).
> Content: praises Germanic monogamy and dowry-from-husband
> (inverting Roman dowry practice).
> Bias: idealizing barbarian-virtue topos; Tacitus uses Germania
> to critique Rome.
> Value: the topos itself reveals Roman elite anxieties about
> moral decline.
> Limitation: not reliable evidence of actual Germanic practice
> in 98 CE — at best filtered through Roman cultural categories.
> Corroboration: archaeological evidence (grave goods, dwelling
> patterns) provides independent check; written Germanic sources
> begin centuries later.
> Significance: foundational text for the Roman "noble savage"
> tradition; subsequently weaponized for nationalist projects in
> 19th-20th c. Germany — read with awareness of its reception
> history, not just composition.

## Discipline rules

- **Do not assume sincerity.** The author may believe what they
  wrote — or may be performing belief.
- **Do not assume accuracy.** Even sincere authors get facts
  wrong; firsthand witnesses often disagree.
- **Do not flatten the source to its propaganda value.**
  Propaganda has internal logic and content beyond its
  manipulative function.
- **Cite the edition.** "Tacitus, *Germania*" is not a citation;
  "Tacitus, *Germania*, trans. Hutton & Peterson, Loeb 1914,
  ch. 18" is.

## Output block

Wrap in `:::artifact` with `template: source-analysis`. Include the
source identifier, edition cited, and the OPCVL fields explicitly.

## Pedagogical scaffolding

When the user is a learner, walk them through one step at a time
— don't dump the whole frame. Have them attempt provenance and
purpose before you fill in. The skill is in the repetition, not
the answer.
